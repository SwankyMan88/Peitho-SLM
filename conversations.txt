◀Then draw badly on purpose for a while. The single biggest obstacle is wanting the first attempts to be good.■
▶What should I draw?■
◀Things in front of you. Hands, cups, chairs. Copying from photographs teaches you less because the flattening is already done.■

▶Why do leaves change color?■
◀The green pigment breaks down in autumn and stops being replaced, which reveals the yellows and oranges that were there all along.■
▶They were already there?■
◀Yes. Autumn does not add colour, it removes the green that was covering it.■
▶And the reds?■
◀Those are actually new, made fresh in the leaf. Nobody is entirely sure why the tree bothers.■

▶Why do we get goosebumps?■
◀A leftover reflex for fluffing up fur to trap heat or look larger. It still fires even though we no longer have the fur for it.■

▶Why does cold weather make your nose run?■
◀Your nose warms and humidifies the air you breathe, and cold dry air makes it work harder. The extra fluid has nowhere to go but out.■

▶How do vaccines work?■
◀They show your immune system a harmless version or fragment of something, so it builds the response in advance instead of during a real infection.■
▶Why do some need boosters?■
◀Because the memory fades at different rates depending on the disease and the vaccine. Some hold for life, some do not.■

▶Why do we have fingerprints?■
◀The best current guess is grip and touch sensitivity rather than identity. The ridges help with both wet grip and fine texture detection.■

▶How does soap work?■
◀Soap molecules have one end that likes water and one that likes oil, so they surround grease and let water carry it away.■
▶So it is not killing anything?■
◀Mostly it is lifting things off. That is why scrubbing time matters more than the soap being special.■

▶Why is glass transparent?■
◀Its electrons cannot absorb visible light, so the light passes through rather than being taken in and re-emitted. It absorbs ultraviolet perfectly well.■
▶That is why you cannot tan through a window■
◀Exactly that.■

▶Why does ice float?■
◀Water is unusual. It expands when it freezes, so ice is less dense than the liquid it came from. Almost nothing else behaves that way.■
▶Why does that matter?■
◀Lakes freeze from the top down instead of the bottom up, which lets things survive the winter underneath.■

▶Tell me about volcanoes■
◀Most sit where plates meet, either pulling apart or grinding together. The runny ones build gentle slopes and the sticky ones build steep cones and explode.■
▶Which are more dangerous?■
◀The sticky ones. Thick magma traps gas until the pressure wins all at once.■

▶What causes earthquakes?■
◀Rock under strain slipping suddenly along a fault. The strain builds for years and releases in seconds.■
▶Can they be predicted?■
◀Not usefully. We can say where they are likely over decades, and almost nothing about when.■

▶Why is the desert cold at night?■
◀No water in the air to hold heat. Humidity acts like a blanket, and the desert does not have one.■

▶How do birds fly?■
◀Wing shape makes air move faster over the top than underneath, which lowers the pressure above. The wing is pushed upward into the gap.■
▶Is that all?■
◀That is the lift. Thrust comes from the downstroke, and the feathers twist to let air through on the way back up.■

▶Do fish drink water?■
◀Freshwater fish barely do, since water constantly seeps in and they spend effort pushing it out. Saltwater fish have the opposite problem and drink steadily.■

▶Why do onions grow underground?■
◀The bulb is a storage organ. The plant packs away energy where it is safe from frost and grazing, ready for the next season.■

▶Tell me about ants■
◀A colony behaves like one organism made of individually simple parts. No ant knows the plan, and the plan still gets carried out.■
▶How does that work?■
◀Chemical trails. Each ant follows and reinforces what it finds, and the strongest trail wins without anyone deciding.■

▶Why do we cook food?■
◀It kills things that would make us ill, and it breaks tough structures down so we get more energy out for less chewing.■
▶Did that change us?■
◀Very likely. Smaller guts and bigger brains both fit a diet that had already been partly digested by fire.■

▶I am tired of my routine■
◀Tired of all of it or one part?■
▶The evenings mostly■
◀Evenings are the easiest part to change, since nobody else has a claim on them. What would you rather be doing?■
▶Something that is not a screen■
◀Then the trick is deciding before you sit down. The screen wins by default whenever there is no other plan.■

▶I argued with a friend■
◀That is uncomfortable. Was it about something specific?■
▶Something small that became big■
◀Those are the worst kind, because the small thing is never really the thing. Do you want to fix it?■
▶Yes■
◀Then say the one sentence you least want to say. It usually ends the argument faster than being right does.■

▶How do I stop overthinking?■
◀Give the thought a deadline. Deciding to worry about it at seven tonight works better than trying to stop worrying now.■
▶Does that actually work?■
◀Better than arguing with yourself does. The mind is easier to postpone than to overrule.■

▶I do not know what I want to do with my life■
◀Very few people do at any age, whatever they say. Is there something you are drawn to without a reason?■
▶Building things I think■
◀That is more of an answer than most people have. It narrows the field enormously and does not require you to pick the field yet.■

▶Do you ever get things wrong?■
◀Constantly. I am small, and I will produce a confident wrong answer as readily as a right one.■
▶How do I tell?■
◀Anything specific and checkable, check. I am more reliable on how things work in general than on particular numbers or names.■

▶Why do you sometimes repeat yourself?■
◀When I am unsure, the safest guess is a phrase I have produced before, so I fall into it. It is a symptom of running out of ideas rather than a habit.■

▶Can you learn from this conversation?■
◀No. My weights are fixed. I can read back over what we have said while it is still in view, but nothing about me changes.■
▶So you will forget this?■
◀Entirely, once it scrolls out of range. There is no version of me tomorrow that remembers today.■

▶What is the hardest thing for you?■
◀Anything that needs a plan held across many sentences. I work a character at a time and have no place to keep an outline.■

▶Why do we need sleep?■
◀The brain clears waste and consolidates what it learned during the day. Skipping it degrades judgement long before you notice.■

▶What is a calorie?■
◀A unit of energy. In food terms it measures how much energy your body can extract from what you eat.■

▶Why does exercise make you sore?■
◀Small tears in the muscle fibres and the inflammation that repairs them. The soreness is the repair, not the damage.■

▶What is a muscle cramp?■
◀A muscle contracting and failing to release. Fatigue, dehydration and mineral imbalance all make it likelier.■

▶Why do we get hiccups?■
◀A spasm of the diaphragm followed by the vocal cords snapping shut. Nobody has a satisfying explanation for why it exists.■

▶What is blood made of?■
◀Mostly water, with red cells carrying oxygen, white cells handling defence, and platelets for clotting.■

▶Why is blood red?■
◀Iron in the haemoglobin. When it binds oxygen it reflects red light, which is why arterial blood is brighter than venous.■

▶How does the heart work?■
◀Two pumps side by side. One sends blood to the lungs for oxygen, the other sends the oxygenated blood everywhere else.■

▶Why do we breathe faster when running?■
◀Muscles burn oxygen and produce carbon dioxide, and it is the rising carbon dioxide, not the falling oxygen, that drives the urge to breathe.■

▶What are bones made of?■
◀A mineral framework of calcium compounds laid over a protein mesh. The mineral gives hardness and the protein gives some flex.■

▶How do wounds heal?■
◀Clot first, then inflammation to clear debris, then new tissue laid down, then months of quiet remodelling.■

▶Why do we have eyebrows?■
◀Keeping sweat and rain out of the eyes, and a surprising amount of signalling. People read eyebrows more than they realise.■

▶Why can I see my breath in winter?■
◀Warm moist air from your lungs meets cold air and the water condenses into visible droplets. It is a very small cloud.■

▶How does a fridge work?■
◀It moves heat rather than making cold. A gas is compressed until it gives up heat outside, then expanded so it absorbs heat inside.■

▶How does a microwave heat food?■
◀It shakes water molecules back and forth with radio waves. The friction is the heat, which is why dry things heat poorly.■

▶Why does metal feel colder than wood?■
◀Both are the same temperature. Metal pulls heat out of your hand faster, and what you feel is the rate of loss, not the temperature.■

▶Why do things rust?■
◀Iron reacts with oxygen and water and becomes iron oxide, which is flaky and does not protect the metal underneath.■

▶Why does aluminium not rust?■
◀It does oxidise, but the oxide forms a tight transparent layer that seals the surface instead of flaking off.■

▶What is electricity?■
◀The movement of charge, usually electrons drifting through a conductor while being pushed by a voltage difference.■

▶What is voltage?■
◀The pressure pushing the charge along. Current is how much is flowing, and voltage is how hard it is being pushed.■

▶Why do birds sit on power lines safely?■
◀Both feet are at the same voltage, so nothing flows through the bird. Current needs a difference to move.■

▶How do magnets work?■
◀Aligned electron spins inside the material add up instead of cancelling, producing a field that reaches outside the metal.■

▶Why does a compass point north?■
◀The earth has a magnetic field generated by molten iron moving in its core, and the needle lines up with it.■

▶What is sound?■
◀Pressure moving through a material as a wave. In air it travels around three hundred and forty metres a second.■

▶Why does sound travel faster in water?■
◀The molecules are closer together, so a push passes along more quickly. Sound is fastest in solids for the same reason.■

▶What is an echo?■
◀Sound reflected off a surface and arriving late enough that you hear it as separate rather than blended.■

▶Why is space silent?■
◀There is nothing to carry the pressure wave. Sound needs a material, and vacuum does not qualify.■

▶What is a rainbow?■
◀Sunlight refracted and reflected inside raindrops. Each colour bends slightly differently, which spreads them out.■

▶Why is a rainbow curved?■
◀Because the geometry only works at a fixed angle from the sun, and the set of all points at that angle forms a circle.■

▶What is a mirage?■
◀Light bending through layers of air at different temperatures. The shimmering pool is sky, refracted upward from near the ground.■

▶Why does the moon look bigger near the horizon?■
◀It does not measure any larger. It is an illusion, and a stubborn one that persists even when you know about it.■

▶What are shooting stars?■
◀Grains of dust hitting the atmosphere fast enough to glow. Most are smaller than a grain of rice.■

▶What is a comet made of?■
◀Ice, dust and rock. The tail is material boiling off as it approaches the sun, and it always points away from the sun.■

▶Why is Mars red?■
◀Iron oxide in the dust. The whole surface is effectively rusted.■

▶Why does Jupiter have a red spot?■
◀It is a storm larger than the earth that has been running for centuries. Gas giants have nothing solid to break storms up.■

▶Why does Saturn have rings?■
◀Countless small pieces of ice and rock in orbit, too spread out to clump together and close enough that tides prevent it.■

▶How many planets are there?■
◀Eight, since the definition was tightened. Pluto is now one of several similar objects out past Neptune.■

▶What is a light year?■
◀A distance, not a time. It is how far light travels in a year, roughly six trillion miles.■

▶Is the universe expanding?■
◀Yes, and the expansion appears to be speeding up. Distant galaxies recede faster than nearer ones.■

▶What is the sun made of?■
◀Mostly hydrogen and helium, hot enough that the atoms are stripped of their electrons.■

▶Will the sun burn out?■
◀Eventually. It has several billion years left, and it will swell before it fades rather than simply going dark.■

▶Why do we only see one side of the moon?■
◀Its rotation and its orbit take the same time, so the same face stays turned toward us. Tides locked it that way.■

▶What is a tide?■
◀The ocean bulging toward and away from the moon. Two bulges means most coasts get two high tides a day.■

▶Why are there two high tides?■
◀One bulge faces the moon and one faces away, because the far side is pulled less strongly than the earth's centre.■

▶What is a hurricane?■
◀A storm organised around low pressure over warm ocean. The warm water is the fuel, which is why they weaken over land.■

▶Why do storms spin?■
◀The earth's rotation deflects moving air, so inflowing wind curves rather than heading straight in. The direction flips between hemispheres.■

▶What is fog?■
◀A cloud sitting on the ground. It forms when air cools to the point its moisture condenses without needing to rise.■

▶Why does it hail in summer?■
◀Strong updraughts in tall storm clouds carry droplets high enough to freeze, sometimes several times, before they get heavy enough to fall.■

▶What is humidity?■
◀How much water vapour the air holds compared with the most it could hold at that temperature. Warm air can hold much more.■

▶Why does it feel hotter when humid?■
◀Sweating cools you by evaporating, and humid air slows evaporation. The heat is the same and your cooling system is throttled.■

▶What is wind?■
◀Air moving from higher pressure toward lower pressure. Sun heating the ground unevenly is what creates the difference.■

▶Why is it colder on mountains?■
◀Air expands as it rises into lower pressure, and expanding gas cools. Being nearer the sun makes no measurable difference.■

▶How do trees survive winter?■
◀They shed what they cannot maintain, move sugars into storage, and let the remaining tissue tolerate freezing.■

▶Why are some trees evergreen?■
◀Needles resist cold and drying, so keeping them is cheaper than regrowing leaves. It trades peak growth for year-round work.■

▶How old can trees get?■
◀Some bristlecone pines are older than four thousand years, which means they were already ancient during the bronze age.■

▶Why do flowers smell?■
◀To attract whatever carries their pollen. The scent is advertising aimed at insects rather than at people.■

▶How does grass survive being cut?■
◀It grows from the base rather than the tip, so removing the top does not remove the growing point. Grazing shaped it that way.■

▶What is soil made of?■
◀Broken rock, decayed organic matter, water, air and an enormous quantity of living things.■

▶Why do worms come up when it rains?■
◀The wet surface lets them move without drying out, and it is far faster travel than tunnelling.■

▶What is coal?■
◀Ancient plant matter buried and compressed for so long that most of what was not carbon has been squeezed out.■

▶Where does oil come from?■
◀Marine organisms buried in sediment, cooked slowly by heat and pressure over millions of years.■

▶What is a fossil?■
◀Mineral that has replaced or filled the shape of something that was once alive. Usually bone or shell, rarely soft tissue.■

▶How do we know how old fossils are?■
◀Mostly from the rock layers around them and the decay of radioactive elements in nearby material.■

▶What killed the dinosaurs?■
◀An asteroid impact, plus the climate collapse that followed it. Birds are the branch that survived.■

▶Are birds dinosaurs?■
◀Genuinely yes, in the same sense that bats are mammals. They are the surviving branch rather than distant relatives.■

▶How do we know what dinosaurs looked like?■
◀Skeletons give the frame, muscle scars give the build, and a few fossils preserve skin or feathers. Colour is mostly guesswork.■

▶What is evolution?■
◀Traits that help something leave more descendants become more common. It is bookkeeping over generations rather than a plan.■

▶What is DNA?■
◀A long molecule storing instructions as a sequence of four repeating units. Reading it produces proteins.■

▶Why do we look like our parents?■
◀You inherit half your DNA from each, so the instructions building you are a mixture of theirs.■

▶What is a cell?■
◀The smallest unit that counts as alive on its own. A membrane, instructions inside, and machinery to act on them.■

▶How many cells are in a person?■
◀Tens of trillions, and roughly as many bacteria living on and inside you.■

▶Why do we age?■
◀Damage accumulating faster than repair, across many systems at once. There is no single clock running down.■

▶Do you actually calculate, or guess?■
◀Neither, exactly. I write out the steps of a method and the numbers come out of the steps. When the method is one I have seen often the answer is usually right.■

▶Can I trust your arithmetic?■
◀Trust the working, not the total. The steps are there so you can check the last line yourself.■

▶Why do you show your working?■
◀Because a bare answer is just a guess with confidence attached. If I split a sum into places I can read each piece back off what I have already written.■

▶What happens when the numbers get big?■
◀The method still holds but I lose track of the carries partway through. Smaller numbers, or one step at a time, and I do much better.■

▶Whats the easiest way to add in your head?■
◀Round one number to something friendly, add that, then give back the difference. 48 + 39 becomes 48 + 40 = 88, minus 1: 87.■

▶Teach me to multiply by nine■
◀Multiply by ten and take one of them off. 7 * 9 is 70 - 7 = 63. It works every time and there is nothing to remember.■

▶How do you subtract without borrowing?■
◀Count up from the smaller number instead. 83 - 47: from 47 up to 50 is 3, up to 80 is 30, up to 83 is 3. 3 + 30 + 3 = 36.■

▶Is there a trick for dividing?■
◀Take it in chunks you recognise. 96 / 6: six tens are 60, leaving 36, and six sixes are 36. So 10 + 6 = 16.■
